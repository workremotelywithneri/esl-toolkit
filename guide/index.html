Your Complete ESL Teacher Guide

From preparation to onboarding — a free, step-by-step manual for Filipino teachers starting out in online ESL. Preparation · Certification · Application · Onboarding.

Welcome!

If you're here, you're probably curious about online ESL teaching or looking for a place to start. I hope this guide makes that process a little easier.

When I was learning about ESL, I spent a lot of time searching through websites, videos, Facebook groups, and discussions just to find reliable information. There was plenty of helpful content out there, but it was often scattered across different places and difficult to piece together.

This guide brings many of those resources together in one place so you can spend less time searching and more time preparing.

Whether you're a fresh graduate, a student, someone looking for extra income, or thinking about a career change, I hope you find something here that helps.

Good luck, and happy learning!

⚠️ A Few Things to Keep in Mind

Everything in this guide is based on publicly available information as of 2026, along with personal research and experience. While I've done my best to keep things accurate and up to date, you should always verify important details for yourself.

ESL companies regularly change their requirements, pay rates, hiring status, and policies—sometimes without notice. Any salary figures mentioned here are estimates, not guarantees. Your actual earnings will depend on factors such as performance, availability, ratings, and the company you work for.

Online ESL can be rewarding, but it isn't always predictable. Companies may pause hiring, change policies, merge with other businesses, or shut down entirely. It's important to approach the industry with realistic expectations.

No company paid to be included in this guide. Any recommendations are based solely on independent research and experience.

Always check a company's official website before applying. Treat this guide as a starting point—not the final word.

Take what's useful, verify what matters, and move at your own pace.

Is ESL Teaching for Me?

Things you should know before becoming an ESL teacher.

What is ESL teaching?

ESL stands for English as a Second Language. ESL teachers help non-native speakers — usually from China, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, and other Asian countries — learn English through one-on-one or group online sessions. As a Filipino teacher, you actually have an edge here — Filipinos are known globally for a neutral accent, solid English, and being naturally easy to talk to.

Who are your students?

  • Chinese children ages 3–18 (most common market)
  • Japanese adults (Engoo, NativeCamp)
  • Korean students (various companies)
  • Adults learning for business or travel purposes

Note: this is a worldwide opportunity — while many positions are in Asia, there's also demand from students in Europe and the US.

What will you teach?

Conversational EnglishPhonics & readingGrammarBusiness EnglishExam prep (IELTS/TOEFL/TOEIC)

While there are specializations, you may want to explore all of them first. In most companies you can't choose your students unless the company caters specifically to one group — most offer a mix of children, teens, and adults, which helps you figure out which category you prefer along the way.

One thing I've noticed: a lot of new teachers think they'll immediately get the age group they prefer. Sometimes you will. Sometimes you won't. 😆 When you're starting out, try to be open to teaching different ages and levels — you might end up loving the group you thought you'd dislike teaching.

Reality check

ESL can be rewarding, flexible, and surprisingly fun — pero let's keep our expectations realistic too. Unlike a regular job, most ESL platforms don't give you a fixed monthly salary. Your income usually depends on student bookings, your schedule, and how you're doing on the platform. There will be months when bookings are great, and months when things slow down. Completely normal sa industry na 'to.

Check your eligibility

Most online ESL companies that hire Filipinos require the following. Please note requirements vary by employer — research the specific companies you're targeting.

College degree

Bachelor's in any field, not limited to Education. Some platforms don't require a degree.

English proficiency

Neutral accent, clear pronunciation, high fluency — no heavy regional accent on camera.

TEFL/TESOL

Minimum 120 hours for most companies. Some strongly prefer it.

NBI Clearance

Required by most companies — make sure it's updated.

Government ID

Any valid Philippine ID: Passport, UMID, PhilHealth, etc.

Age

Most require at least 18 years old; some prefer 21+.

Stable internet

10–25 Mbps wired for most companies. Wi-Fi often acceptable, but some are strict about Ethernet.

Computer/laptop

Windows 10+ or macOS. Specs vary per company.

Optional but recommended: a backup power supply and mobile data. Some companies penalize downtime — a prepaid broadband connection, hotspot, and power bank/generator are worth investing in over time, not necessarily on day one.

You do not need to be a licensed professional teacher (LET passer) — though a PRC license is a major advantage.

Good to know

Teaching experience: some companies ask for a Certificate of Employment (COE) from either online ESL or face-to-face experience. Have your documentation ready if you have any.

BIR COR: most Japanese companies require a Certificate of Registration from the BIR. Many Chinese, Korean, and European companies don't — but if a Japanese company doesn't get a COR from you, they may deduct 10% automatically for tax.

Employment status: you're usually an independent contractor or self-employed unless your contract says otherwise. Read contracts carefully to understand your tax obligations.

Get Certified

TEFL/TESOL options, free links, and recommendations.

One of the most common questions I get is: "Kailangan ko ba talaga ng TEFL or TESOL?" Honestly, it depends on the company. Some will hire you without one, while others won't even let you continue your application.

If you have the budget, I recommend getting at least a 120-hour certificate. Aside from helping with applications, you'll also pick up techniques that make your first few classes a lot less intimidating.

Note

Please take the course yourself if you decide to get certified. I know life gets busy, and I know there are people online offering "shortcuts" — pero the real value isn't the certificate itself, it's what you actually learn while earning it. Especially kung bago ka pa lang, you'll thank yourself later.

Where I actually learned most of my skills

If I'm being honest, I learned more from actual classes than from any certificate. Certificates taught me the basics. Students taught me the rest.

My first few classes were awkward. I talked too fast. I overexplained things. Sometimes I couldn't tell if the student understood me or was just being polite. 😭 That's normal — every ESL teacher starts somewhere. Don't wait until you feel 100% ready, kasi that day never really comes.

Frequently asked

Do I really need these certificates?

No, if the company you're applying to doesn't require it. But if it does, budget the 120 hours and get it early so you're more prepared — you can use the certificate for a lifetime, and the skills are worth it, especially as a newbie figuring out what ESL teaching really is.

I'm not an English Education major — can I still get certified?

Yes. Anyone can get certified as long as you take the course and pay for it — you don't need an Education background.

Set Up Your Space

Tech requirements, equipment guide, and documents to prepare.

Your background and setup will be evaluated during your demo class and introduction video — make sure everything looks and sounds professional.

Computer / laptop

Windows 10+ or macOS. At least Intel Core i3 8th gen or AMD Ryzen, 8GB RAM minimum for most platforms.

Internet connection

Stable matters more than super-fast. I've seen teachers with high-speed plans struggle because their connection kept dropping, while others with lower speeds did just fine because it was reliable. Most companies want wired DSL or Fiber (10–25 Mbps) — no mobile hotspot or LTE.

Webcam

Built-in laptop cam works for beginners; an external HD webcam is a plus.

Headset / mic

USB or 3.5mm headset with a clear mic to reduce background noise.

Lighting

Natural light in front of you, or a ring light. Avoid dark rooms and backlighting.

Background

Plain wall, bookshelf, or simple color. Clean and professional; colorful-but-tidy for kids' classes.

Noise environment

Quiet room — no barking dogs, loud fans, or TV noise during class.

Note

Background requirements vary a lot depende sa ina-applyan niyo — may companies na gusto ng bongga ang background lalo na kung kids, pero kung adults naman, basta clean o plain white, keri na. Always check their guidelines first before you spend on decorations. Nakakahinayang bumili tapos hindi naman pala required. 😭

Most companies prefer a headset with noise-canceling features. If your environment is noisy — traffic, dogs, crying babies, karaoke — apps like Krisp or SteelSeries Sonar (Windows) can help keep your audio clean.

Prepare your documents

Have these ready in digital format (PDF or JPG) before you apply:

  • Diploma or Transcript of Records (TOR) — scanned, clear copy
  • NBI Clearance — updated, not expired (if needed)
  • Government-issued ID — passport preferred for international platforms
  • TEFL/TESOL Certificate — PDF from your course provider
  • Resume/CV — 1–2 pages, include teaching or customer service experience
  • Introduction video — 1–3 minutes
  • PayPal, bank, or GCash account — for receiving salary (depends on company)
💡 Tip

Scan documents at 200–300 DPI and save as PDF. Keep them in a folder labeled "ESL Application Documents." No scanner? Use your phone: on iPhone, open Files → long press → Scan Documents. On Android, check the Play Store for a scanning app without a watermark on the free version.

Intro Video Guide

What to include, a sample script, and free editing tools.

What is an introduction video?

A short (1–3 minute) self-introduction video where you speak directly to the camera. Students and recruiters watch this to evaluate your personality, voice clarity, professionalism, and teaching energy before booking or hiring you.

Note

Almost every ESL platform requires an intro video — wala pa akong na-encounter na company na hindi humihingi nito. No pressure ha, pero this is honestly one of the most important parts of your application. Recruiters and students usually see this before they ever meet you, kaya it can really make or break the first impression. Galingan mo talaga dito!

Nail your pronunciation

Start with tongue twisters: "Peter Piper picked a peck of pickled peppers." "Thirty-three thieves thought they thrilled the throne throughout Thursday." "Four furious friends fought for the phone."

Then double-check these common sound swaps in your recording:

P ↔ F

"Coffee" → "kopi," "Family" → "pamily," "Professor" → "propessor" — the Abakada traditionally lacked the F sound.

B ↔ V

"Victory" → "bictory," "Video" → "bideo."

TH sound

"Thin" → "tin," "This/that" → "dis/dat," "Mother" → "mader" — we don't really use that TH sound in everyday Filipino, so it doesn't come naturally at first.

Short i vs. long e

"Ship" vs "sheep," "fill" vs "feel," "it" vs "eat" often collapse into one sound.

S vs Z

"Please" can come out as "pleass," "hazard" as "hasard."

Schwa reduction

"Chocolate" (chok-lit) pronounced fully as "cho-ko-late"; "comfortable" as "com-for-ta-ble."

Note

Confession time: this was probably the sound I struggled with the most when I was starting. Nangalay talaga ang panga ko sa dami ng practice sa tongue twisters, huhu. Pero the good news? Nasanay na rin ako — it gets easier with repetition.

Note

Recently lang, a student's parent told my employer na "pen-sils" daw ang sabi ko instead of "PEN-suhlz" — yung "ci" kasi gumagawa ng soft "s" sound, tapos yung ending na "-s" pronounced as /z/, kaya dapat "PEN-suhlz" talaga ang tunog, hindi "pen-sils." Sa casual conversation, keri lang 'yan, pero sa ESL world, medyo strict talaga sila sa mga sounds na 'to. Minsan hindi ko na rin namamalayan na mali pala lagi kong sinasabi ang isang word, lalo na sa mabilis na usapan. Pronunciation standards can get really strict depending on the student, parent, or company — you'll get feedback on sounds you've been saying the same way your whole life. Don't take it personally. Treat it as part of the job and keep improving. Expect the unexpected talaga sa ESL world.

Production tips

  • Keep it 1–3 minutes for company profiles
  • Speak slowly and clearly — your students are non-native speakers
  • Smile throughout — energy matters more than perfection
  • Use natural hand gestures and look at the camera, not the screen
  • Film in a quiet, well-lit room; natural daylight in front of you is best, ring light if filming at night
  • Dress professionally — smart casual, avoid bold patterns
  • Use a plain, clean background (a bookshelf or light wall works great)
  • Test your audio before filming — no echo
  • Write a script and practice 5–10 times — you don't need to memorize it word-for-word
  • Use props, especially if applying to a company where most students are children

What to include (sample structure)

  1. 1

    Opening / greeting — 0–15 sec

    Greet warmly with a big smile. "Hi! My name is [Name] and I'm a teacher from the Philippines!"

  2. 2

    Your background — 15–40 sec

    Mention your degree, TEFL/TESOL certification, and relevant experience.

  3. 3

    Your teaching style — 40–80 sec

    Patient? Fun? Structured? Do you use visuals, games, or conversation?

  4. 4

    Who you teach — 80–100 sec

    Young learners? Adults? Beginners? Mention the students you're excited to work with.

  5. 5

    Closing / CTA — 100–120 sec

    Invite students to book a class. "I can't wait to help you on your English learning journey!"

Note

No teaching experience? Don't panic — a lot of ESL teachers started with zero classroom experience. My coach once told me to just say, "I have been teaching for several years," without specifying the subject. I was a Science teacher for 7 years, kaya medyo ligwak ako pag sinabi kong Science ang tinuruan ko sa isang English class — so I just said I'd been teaching for a while, in general. It helped me get hired when I was starting out. If you truly have zero experience, focus on your certificates, communication skills, customer service background, or anything that shows you can explain things clearly and work well with people.

Sample script template

"Hi! My name is [First Name] and I'm an English teacher from the Philippines. I have a Bachelor's degree in [Course] and I hold TESOL and TEFL certifications. I specialize in teaching [young learners / conversational English / business English / exam prep]. My lessons are interactive, engaging, and tailored to each student's needs — I believe every learner is unique, and I make sure each session is fun and productive. Whether you're a complete beginner or looking to polish your fluency, I'm here to support your journey. Book a trial lesson with me today — I look forward to learning with you!"

Application Process

Step-by-step application, demo tips, and interview tips.

Every company has a slightly different process, but the general flow is the same:

  1. 1

    Research & Choose

    Decide which company/ies to apply to. Check if you meet their requirements. Visit their official website.

  2. 2

    Create an Account

    Register on the platform's teacher/tutor portal. Fill in your profile completely.

  3. 3

    Submit Documents

    Upload your diploma, NBI clearance, valid ID, TEFL/TESOL certificate. Some platforms ask for your intro video at this stage.

  4. 4

    Complete Application Form

    Answer questions about your teaching background, availability, tech setup.

  5. 5

    Online Interview

    Some companies do a Skype/Zoom interview. Other companies (51 talk and TutorABC) use an AI video interview.

  6. 6

    Demo / Mock Class

    You teach a sample lesson to an evaluator or AI. Use the company's provided materials. Be energetic, clear, and use props/visuals. (It can be a live demo or a recording that you will submit.)

  7. 7

    Training

    After passing, most companies provide onboarding training on their platform, teaching methods, and tools.

  8. 8

    Set Your Schedule

    Open your available time slots. For most companies, peak hours (evening PH time / weekend) get more bookings.

  9. 9

    First Classes

    Students book your time. Your first few classes are crucial for getting ratings and repeat bookings.

Tips for a successful demo class

  • Practice the sample lesson multiple times before the actual demo.
  • Use TPR (Total Physical Response): point, gesture, use facial expressions.
  • Use a whiteboard or show flashcards for visual support during the lesson.
  • Speak slowly, check for understanding, repeat important words.
  • Be warm and encouraging — pretend a real student is there (There are companies that ask for a real recorded class from your previous employers; this is for experienced teachers)
  • If teaching kids: use a puppet, make animal sounds, keep it playful.
  • If teaching adults: be professional and conversational.
  • In most Japanese and Chinese companies, you can type what was asked or the words being learned during class. This will help the students remember the material better.
Note

Give food rewards tapos may crunch sound pa minsan, HAHAHA. Kids love reactions — minsan hindi pa nga importante yung reward mismo, gusto lang nila makita kang excited kasama nila. Mas playful, mas okay! Pero yan ha, sa kids lang — hindi sa adults, XD

Note

I-enjoy mo lang ang demo. Laruin mo lang rin yung kids. Get their attention.

Tip: Search "demo class for [your target company]" on YouTube to watch real teacher demos before yours. I have sample videos below too.

Interview tips

  • Dress professionally (at least collared shirt or blouse).
  • Test your internet, camera, and microphone 30 minutes before the interview.
  • Have your documents open and ready to share if asked.
  • Speak clearly and confidently. Smile.
  • Prepare to answer: Why do you want to teach? What is your teaching style? What age group do you prefer?
Note

Ang paghahanap ng company ay parang paghahanap ng tamang tao — kung para sa'yo, para sa'yo talaga. Rejections happen, kahit qualified ka naman. Minsan may company na akala mo bagay sa'yo pero hindi ka tinanggap, tapos biglang matatanggap ka naman sa company na hindi mo talaga inexpect. Wag ka masyadong mag-attach sa isang company lang. Keep applying nang keep applying — makukuha mo rin ang tamang company para sa'yo.

After You're Hired

Onboarding & what to expect.

Note

Congratulations — seriously. Ayan! Whether hired ka na sa ngayon, or preparing ka pa lang, either way, mas malayo ka na kaysa noong sinimulan mong basahin itong guide na 'to. Alam kong nakakapagod ang application process, lalo na kung first time mo. I-claim mo na 'yan — you are an official ESL teacher na. Get ready sa mga classes mo!

  1. 1

    Go through onboarding training

    Most companies will run you through some training first — videos, modules, sometimes a quick assessment. (Kung meron man — depende sa company.)

  2. 2

    Set up your payment account

    PayPal, Payoneer, or local bank — varies per company.

  3. 3

    Finalize your teacher profile

    Photo, intro video, bio, specializations.

  4. 4

    Open your available schedule

    More hours open = more chances for bookings.

  5. 5

    Prepare your teaching materials

    Flashcards, props, stickers (for kids), whiteboard.

  6. 6

    Do your first trial class

    Do your first trial class with your first student. Galingan mo every trial class para ma-convert into regular student mo. Pero... yan na ang pero... not all trial classes will convert into regular classes. Yung iba, after a month or a year pa gusto mag-sign up, at ang iba naman, maghahanap ng ibang regular teachers. Don't lose hope; just do your best in every class, and the students will come.

Setting expectations

Earning timeline

Most new teachers earn little in the first 1–2 months. Bookings increase as you build ratings and a student base. Be patient.

Peak hours

You will need to open availability during evening and weekend hours (Philippines time) because most students are in China, Japan, or Korea (different time zones).

Ratings matter

Every session, students rate you. High ratings = more bookings. Always give 100% even if the session is only 25 minutes.

No bookings ≠ no income

Most platforms are 'per class' — if no student books you, you earn nothing.

Multi-platform strategy

Many successful teachers apply to 2–3 platforms simultaneously to maximize income and stability.

Note

One thing I wish someone told me earlier: mababang bookings don't automatically mean you're a bad teacher. Minsan mabagal lang talaga ang platform. Minsan holiday season. Minsan nagtitipid lang ang mga magulang. Wag kang masyadong maging mabigat sa sarili mo.

Note

Companies lang pinagsasabay ha, hindi tao!

How to grow as an ESL teacher

  1. 1

    Ask for student feedback after every class. If there is a rating system, you can politely ask the student to rate you on the platform. Most parents send their ratings, especially if nagustuhan talaga nila yung teacher.

  2. 2

    Upgrade your setup. Better lighting, faster internet, and an HD webcam. Paunti-unti lang siyempre—gradually provide equipment that can improve your setup.

  3. 3

    Get specialized certifications (TEFL for Business English, TEYL for Young Learners, or IELTS prep).

  4. 4

    Stay active on your platform — log in regularly and keep your schedule updated. Some platforms have challenges or tasks, like Polly English and Lingostar, so try to participate.

  5. 5

    Join Facebook groups for Filipino ESL teachers (ESL communities) for tips, updates, and community support. Remember that this is optional, and be careful din sa pag-join sa groups. There are good and bad people and teachers out there who can either ruin or boost your confidence, especially when you are just asking questions about things you don't know.

Search "Online ESL Teachers Philippines" on Facebook.

Payment & taxes

  • Most platforms pay monthly via bank transfer, PayPal, or Payoneer.
  • ESL income from foreign companies is generally subject to Philippine income tax.
  • Consult a CPA or BIR guidelines on how to declare freelance/online income properly.

Registering as a self-employed professional with the BIR may be required depending on your income level. Do your research or consult a professional.

Real Talk & FAQs

Let's keep it real — here's what nobody tells you upfront.

This industry has no guaranteed stability — and that's okay if you go in prepared

Companies can suspend operations without warning — it has happened before and it will happen again. Chinese government policies on foreign education platforms have directly affected Filipino teachers' income in the past. Bookings can drop significantly during holidays, especially Chinese New Year and Golden Week. Your income is not fixed — it depends entirely on how many students book you, your ratings, and the platform's current performance.

None of this is said to scare you. It's said so you go in with your eyes open.

So how do you earn more?

Higher pay is absolutely possible, but it doesn't happen overnight. It usually comes down to:

  • The company tier you're in
  • Your student ratings and performance scores
  • How many peak hours you open (evenings and weekends = more bookings)
  • Your certifications and specializations
  • Simply, how long you've been on the platform

I've seen amazing teachers struggle with bookings, and I've also seen average teachers become fully booked. Timing, luck, platform algorithms, seasonality, student demand — lahat 'yan may kinalaman. Focus na lang sa kontrolado mo: yung pagtuturo mo, profile mo, availability mo, at yung attitude mo sa pag-aaral at paglago.

The honest advice

Don't treat this as your main income source until you've built a stable student base. Always keep a financial cushion for the slow months.

The reality of ESL seasons

ESL teaching is not for the faint of heart. Paldo kung paldo talaga — there are peak seasons where teachers earn really well. But may matumal months din — lean periods happen due to holidays, student cancellations, or global situations. We see this with platforms like Novakid, where global events cause some students to pause while others continue. There are also cases where teachers get terminated unexpectedly — it's rare but it happens.

The golden rule: do not rely on just one company unless you have complete faith in them. Always have a backup plan.

The same is true for most freelance careers

Think about it — freelance video editors, graphic designers, virtual assistants, content writers, even photographers. None of them have a guaranteed monthly salary either; their income depends on clients, projects, and seasons too. ESL teaching sits in the same category: higher risk, but higher earning potential compared to a fixed-salary job. The trade-off is the same across all of these fields — you give up stability in exchange for flexibility, growth, and the ability to eventually set your own terms.

The difference with ESL is that the barrier to entry is lower than most. You don't need a portfolio, a studio, or years of client work to start — just a stable connection, a good setup, and the willingness to show up consistently.

So if you're already okay with the freelance mindset, you're already halfway there.

Why I Created This Guide

The story behind everything you just read.

I was a high school teacher for seven years before I decided to leave my private school job because of an opportunity that I wanted to pursue.

As a panganay who always wanted the best for my family, I thought it was finally my chance to build a better future for us.

I spent months applying for overseas opportunities in different countries in the Middle East.

Months passed.

I waited.

Prepared.

Hoped.

Prayed.

And honestly, I had already convinced myself that I would be leaving soon.

I prepared mentally, emotionally, and financially for that next chapter of my life. I was already imagining how I could help my family more and finally achieve some of the goals I had been working so hard for.

Then the war happened.

The application process became slower and slower until eventually I started receiving deferred notices.

One.

After another.

After another.

For a while, it felt like everything was finally falling into place. There were interviews, requirements, offers, and plans for a future I thought was already waiting for me. Then everything changed.

Eventually, I decided not to pursue the opportunities anymore for the peace of mind of my family.

I was in a big slump.

At that time, I was already teaching as an ESL teacher. My first companies were Polly English and Jiaozi Tutor. Around the same time, I was also feeling burned out. I eventually resigned because I had no stable internet connection in the province and, honestly, I had no direction in life anymore.

I tried applying again, and eventually another ESL company gave me an opportunity.

When I was teaching, I was okay.

In class, I was my usual self—makwela, madaldal, and always joking around with my students. I genuinely enjoyed teaching, especially kids. Their energy made it easier to smile, laugh, and forget my problems for a little while.

But whenever I didn't have classes, especially during non-peak hours, it was a different story.

I would go back to my bed, curl up, sleep, and spend hours thinking about everything that had happened to me.

I kept replaying the situation in my head.

Wondering what went wrong.

Wondering what I could have done differently.

As a panganay, what hurt the most wasn't just losing an opportunity. It was grieving the future that I thought was already waiting for me.

My overseas application wasn't just another application. It was my biggest hope at that time. I truly believed that it would allow me to help my family more, provide a better future, and finally give back to the people who sacrificed so much for me.

So when everything suddenly stopped, it felt like the future I had been preparing for disappeared overnight.

That was probably the hardest part.

I wasn't only grieving an opportunity.

I was grieving the life that I thought was already waiting for me.

Teaching became one of the few things that distracted me from the grief. For a few hours every day, I wasn't thinking about deferred applications, failed plans, or how lost I felt. I was simply helping students learn.

Looking back now, I think teaching helped me heal little by little, even when I didn't realize it at the time.

Then something unexpected happened.

I fell in love with teaching.

Especially teaching kids.

To be honest, before entering the ESL industry, I wasn't really into teaching kids. Maybe there's a reason why life brought me here.

Maybe I also needed to improve my English-speaking skills. I may not sound like a native speaker, and I'm still far from perfect, but through consistently talking to myself, recording my voice, attending English training sessions, taking free online courses, and even talking to native speakers online just to practice, I improved little by little.

I'm still far from perfect, but I can confidently say that I've improved a lot compared to who I was before entering the ESL world.

Even before resigning from my teaching job, I always liked challenging myself and exploring what I was capable of. But after what happened with my applications, I felt completely lost.

My former colleagues and friends would often ask me what I had been doing all this time. It probably looked like I was hiding from everyone. But the truth was, I was just busy grieving.

Since I didn't have enough time to explain everything to every person who asked, I decided to create this guide.

I first started building it on Google Sites.

Then I thought, why not improve it and make it even better?

At the same time, I started building things again. Little by little, I created resources I wished I had when I was starting out.

Creating this guide gave me something to look forward to. It became one of the reasons I was excited to get up in the morning, face my laptop, and keep going instead of staying in bed and overthinking everything.

It reminded me that I still had a purpose.

That I could still share what I learned from the ESL industry.

That I could still help people who were just starting out.

Sounds dramatic, pero yun talaga. This guide became a light during a time when I felt completely lost.

At first, this guide was simply my way of answering the same questions that my former colleagues, friends, and aspiring ESL teachers kept asking me. But as I continued building it, it became something more.

It became part of my healing process.

It gave me something to work on.

Something to improve.

Something to look forward to. And for the first time in a long while, I found myself excited again.

If you're feeling lost right now, too

So if you're starting out, or if you're like me and feel lost in life right now, I just want you to know that you will get there. There will come a time when you'll have a reason to get up again. There will come a time when you'll be excited about life again. For me, the ESL industry helped me find that reason.

Maybe it won't happen immediately. Maybe not this year. Maybe not even after a few years. But keep going.

Back when I was still teaching in private school, I was desperate to leave and build a different life for myself. Even though I wasn't sure what was ahead, I still took the risk.

The funny thing is, the risk I took didn't give me what I originally wanted.

But it gave me what I needed.

A quote that resonates with me now

"God will not always give you what you want, but He will give you what you need."

I hope the same will be true for you. Trust Him. There will always be opportunities waiting for you, even if you can't see them yet.

I'm still far from perfect.

I'm still learning.

I'm still building this toolkit and creating free resources for ESL teachers.

Everything you see here is built from my own experiences, lessons, mistakes, and things I wish someone had told me when I was starting out.

If this guide helps even one person find direction, save time, avoid mistakes, or gain confidence, then all the hours spent creating it will be worth it.

So wherever you are in your journey right now, keep going. Your story isn't over yet.

And who knows? Maybe one day you'll look back and realize that the thing that broke your plans was also the thing that led you exactly where you needed to be.

Thank you for being here.

And please stay tuned.

— Neri
