Is ESL Teaching For Me?

Things you should know before becoming an ESL teacher.

What Is ESL Teaching?

ESL means English as a Second Language. ESL teachers help people who are learning English practice speaking, listening, reading, pronunciation, and everyday communication—often through one-on-one online classes.

Many students are from China, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, and other parts of Asia, but learners also come from Europe, North America, South America, and elsewhere. Filipino teachers often bring strong English skills, warmth, and an easygoing way of connecting with students.

Who Are Your Students?

It depends on the company and the kind of classes it offers. You might teach:

  • Children and teenagers
  • Japanese or Korean adults
  • Business professionals
  • Travelers preparing for a trip
  • Students studying for an English exam
  • People who want to feel more confident using English every day

My Experience

The youngest student I taught was 2 years old. The oldest was 80. My 2-year-old students were with a Chinese ESL company, and my oldest learner came from a Japanese company.

Many people think ESL teachers only teach children, but that's not always true. Depending on the company, you may teach toddlers, teens, college students, working adults, retirees, or a mix. Every student is different, so classes rarely feel exactly the same.

What Will You Teach?

Lessons can cover conversational English, phonics and reading, grammar, business English, or exam preparation such as IELTS, TOEFL, and TOEIC.

Most companies give new teachers a mix of ages and levels. You may not get to choose your students right away. I wasn't interested in teaching children before I joined ESL, but now it's one of my favorite parts of the job. Give different learners a chance—you may surprise yourself.

A Quick Reality Check

ESL can be flexible and rewarding, but most platforms don't promise a fixed monthly salary. Your income often depends on bookings, your available hours, and company demand. Some months are busy; others are quiet. If you prefer a predictable paycheck, think about that before you begin.

You'll know whether you enjoy the work by trying it. Some people love it; others decide it's not for them. Both are okay. This guide is here to help you make an informed choice.

Check Your Eligibility

Requirements differ from one company to another, so check the jobs you're interested in. Many companies that hire Filipino teachers ask for some of these:

College degree

A bachelor's degree is preferred by many companies, but it doesn't always have to be in Education. Some companies don't require one.

English communication

Clear pronunciation and confidence speaking English matter more than sounding like a native speaker.

TEFL or TESOL

Many employers ask for a certificate, often with at least 120 hours of training. Others don't require one.

NBI Clearance and ID

Some companies ask for an updated NBI Clearance and a valid government ID.

Age and experience

Many companies require applicants to be at least 18. Teaching experience or a teaching license may help, but isn't always required.

Internet and computer

A stable connection and a computer that can run video calls are essential. Exact speed and device requirements vary.

You don't need a teaching license for every ESL job. A PRC license may be an advantage, and some companies ask for proof of past teaching work. Keep a Certificate of Employment handy if you have one.

Some Japanese companies ask for a BIR Certificate of Registration. Many online ESL teachers work as independent contractors, so read your contract and check what the company requires. For tax questions, use current BIR guidance or ask a qualified professional.

You can add backup internet and power as your budget allows. You don't need to buy everything before you apply.

TEFL/TESOL Certificate Guide

TEFL/TESOL options, free links, and recommendations.

One of the most common questions I get is: "Kailangan ko ba talaga ng TEFL or TESOL?" Honestly, it depends on the company. Some will hire you without one, while others won't even let you continue your application.

If you have the budget, I recommend getting at least a 120-hour certificate. Aside from helping with applications, you'll also pick up techniques that make your first few classes a lot less intimidating.

Note

Please take the course yourself if you decide to get certified. I know life gets busy, and I know there are people online offering "shortcuts" — but the real value isn't the certificate itself; it's what you actually learn while earning it. If you're new to teaching, you'll thank yourself later.

Where I actually learned most of my skills

If I'm being honest, I learned more from actual classes than from any certificate. Certificates taught me the basics. Students taught me the rest.

My first few classes were awkward. I talked too fast. I overexplained things. Sometimes I couldn't tell if the student understood me or was just being polite. 😭 That's normal — every ESL teacher starts somewhere. Don't wait until you feel 100% ready, kasi that day never really comes.

Frequently asked

Do I really need these certificates?

No, if the company you're applying to doesn't require it. But if it does, budget the 120 hours and get it early so you're more prepared — you can use the certificate for a lifetime, and the skills are worth it, especially as a newbie figuring out what ESL teaching really is.

I'm not an English Education major — can I still get certified?

Yes. Anyone can get certified as long as you take the course and pay for it — you don't need an Education background.

Expectations vs Reality

A friendly reality check before you jump in—because the job can be rewarding, but it isn’t always what the ads make it look like.

It can be flexible, but you still need a routine

Online ESL can fit around family, studies, or another job. But “work from home” doesn’t mean every hour is available or every class is booked. Your schedule, student demand, and company rules all matter.

Your first month may feel awkward

You’ll be learning the platform, getting used to the lesson materials, and finding your teacher voice. That’s normal. Confidence grows with practice, one class at a time.

Pay and bookings can change

Some weeks are busier than others, and a listed hourly rate doesn’t guarantee a full schedule. Check how a company handles cancellations, penalties, and pay before you commit.

My honest advice: Start with realistic expectations, ask current teachers questions, and keep a backup plan while you build experience.

Set Up Your Space

Tech requirements, equipment guide, and documents to prepare.

Your background and setup will be evaluated during your demo class and introduction video — make sure everything looks and sounds professional.

Computer / laptop

Windows 10+ or macOS. At least Intel Core i3 8th gen or AMD Ryzen, 8GB RAM minimum for most platforms.

Internet connection

Stable matters more than super-fast. I've seen teachers with high-speed plans struggle because their connection kept dropping, while others with lower speeds did just fine because it was reliable. Most companies want wired DSL or Fiber (10–25 Mbps) — no mobile hotspot or LTE.

Webcam

Built-in laptop cam works for beginners; an external HD webcam is a plus.

Headset / mic

USB or 3.5mm headset with a clear mic to reduce background noise.

Lighting

Natural light in front of you, or a ring light. Avoid dark rooms and backlighting.

Background

Plain wall, bookshelf, or simple color. Clean and professional; colorful-but-tidy for kids' classes.

Noise environment

Quiet room — no barking dogs, loud fans, or TV noise during class.

Note

Background requirements vary a lot depende sa ina-applyan niyo — may companies na gusto ng bongga ang background lalo na kung kids, pero kung adults naman, basta clean o plain white, keri na. Always check their guidelines first before you spend on decorations. Nakakahinayang bumili tapos hindi naman pala required. 😭

Most companies prefer a headset with noise-canceling features. If your environment is noisy — traffic, dogs, crying babies, karaoke — apps like Krisp or SteelSeries Sonar (Windows) can help keep your audio clean.

Prepare your documents

Have these ready in digital format (PDF or JPG) before you apply:

  • Diploma or Transcript of Records (TOR) — scanned, clear copy
  • NBI Clearance — updated, not expired (if needed)
  • Government-issued ID — passport preferred for international platforms
  • TEFL/TESOL Certificate — PDF from your course provider
  • Resume/CV — 1–2 pages, include teaching or customer service experience
  • Introduction video — 1–3 minutes
  • PayPal, bank, or GCash account — for receiving salary (depends on company)
💡 Tip

Scan documents at 200–300 DPI and save as PDF. Keep them in a folder labeled "ESL Application Documents." No scanner? Use your phone: on iPhone, open Files → long press → Scan Documents. On Android, check the Play Store for a scanning app without a watermark on the free version.

Intro Video Guide

What to include, what recruiters look for, and a few things I learned while recording my own videos.

What Is an Introduction Video?

Many ESL companies ask for a short video before they interview or hire you. Think of it as a first introduction. Recruiters—and sometimes students—want to hear how you communicate and see whether you'd be a good fit for their classes. You don't need to sound like a native speaker or a news presenter. Be clear, comfortable, and yourself.

A Note From Me

I hate hearing my own voice in recordings. 😆 The first few times, I kept deleting my videos because I noticed so many mistakes. But listening back helped me catch pronunciation habits I didn't notice while speaking. If your first recording makes you cringe a little, you're in good company. Take your time; this video is important, but it doesn't have to be perfect.

Tongue Twisters Practice

Try these to warm up your mouth before recording. You don't have to say them perfectly.

  • Peter Piper picked a peck of pickled peppers.
  • Thirty-three thieves thought they thrilled the throne throughout Thursday.
  • Four furious friends fought for the phone.

Then Double-Check These Common Sound Swaps in Your Recording

After you record, watch your video and listen for patterns that are easy to miss while speaking. Use these examples as a checklist, not a reason to criticize every little thing.

P ↔ F

“Coffee” may sound like “kopi,” or “family” like “pamily.”

B ↔ V

“Victory” may sound like “bictory,” or “video” like “bideo.”

TH sound

“Thin” may sound like “tin,” “this” like “dis,” or “mother” like “mader.” The sound takes practice for many Filipino speakers.

Short I vs. long E

Listen to the difference in “ship” and “sheep,” “fill” and “feel.”

S vs. Z

“Please” may sound like “pleass,” or “hazard” like “hasard.”

Schwa reduction

In natural speech, “chocolate” and “comfortable” are often said with fewer, softer syllables.

Personal Experience

Confession time: TH was the sound I struggled with most when I started. I repeated tongue twisters so many times that my jaw got tired. With practice, the sound started to feel more natural.

Feedback Can Be Unexpected

Recently, a student's parent told my employer that I pronounced “pencils” more like “pen-sils” than “PEN-suhlz.” I hadn't noticed it myself.

In everyday conversation, people may not mind, but pronunciation expectations can be stricter in ESL. You might get feedback on a word you've said the same way your whole life. Don't take it personally—use it to keep learning.

Production Tips

You don't need an expensive setup. Clear sound, good lighting, and a comfortable delivery matter most.

  • Keep it around 1–3 minutes, unless the company asks for something else.
  • Speak clearly and at a comfortable pace.
  • Smile naturally and look at the camera.
  • Record in a quiet room with good light.
  • Wear something neat and professional.
  • Test your sound before you record.
  • Practice your script a few times; you don't have to memorize it.
  • If you're applying to teach children, a simple prop can help.

What to Include

  1. 1

    Greeting

    Say your name and where you're from. “Hi! My name is [Name], and I'm from the Philippines.”

  2. 2

    Background

    Briefly mention your degree, teaching experience, or TEFL/TESOL certificate.

  3. 3

    Teaching style

    Share what your classes are like—friendly, patient, interactive, or structured.

  4. 4

    Who you teach

    Say which students you'd enjoy working with, such as children, adults, or beginners.

  5. 5

    Closing

    End with a warm line like, “I hope to see you in class soon.”

No Teaching Experience Yet?

Don't panic. Many ESL teachers start without ESL experience. I taught Science for seven years, so when a coach suggested I mention my teaching experience, I could honestly say I'd been teaching for several years. 😆 If you're new to teaching, talk about your certificates, communication skills, customer service, public speaking, or other experience working with people.

Application Process

Step-by-step application process, demo tips, and interview advice.

The application process can feel intimidating when you're starting out. Most ESL companies follow a similar flow, even if the details differ.

Some companies move faster.

Some ask for more documents.

Some have interviews, while others focus on demos or recorded videos.

Here's what you can usually expect.

  1. 1

    Research and choose

    Check the company's requirements, student market, schedule, and platform. Visit its official website when possible.

  2. 2

    Create an account

    Register on the teacher or tutor portal. Complete your profile carefully; students may look at it before booking.

  3. 3

    Submit documents

    You may need a diploma or TOR, government ID, NBI Clearance, TEFL/TESOL certificate, or intro video. Requirements vary.

  4. 4

    Complete the application form

    Companies may ask about your experience, certificates, availability, internet, and equipment. Take your time with your answers.

  5. 5

    Interview

    Some companies interview on Zoom, Skype, or another platform. Others use recorded or AI-based interviews. They usually want to see how you communicate and present yourself.

  6. 6

    Demo class

    You may teach a live class or send a recording. Most companies provide materials. They want to see how you interact with a student—not a perfect performance.

  7. 7

    Training

    If you pass, training may cover the platform, class procedures, teaching standards, and company policies.

  8. 8

    Set your schedule

    Once your account is active, open the hours you can keep. Evenings and weekends may be busy for some companies.

  9. 9

    Teach your first classes

    Your first classes help you get used to the platform and meet your students. Everyone starts somewhere.

Platform Preparation

Companies hiring through their own websites often have their own teaching platform. Companies you find through Facebook groups may use ClassIn, Voov Meeting, Zoom, or Tencent Meeting.

Find out which platform you'll need and try it before your interview or demo.

I once confidently told an interviewer I knew how to use ClassIn.

I didn't. 😆

I ended up learning it the day before my demo. Learn from my mistake: watch a tutorial and click around ahead of time.

Some companies also use WeChat, so setting up an account early may save you time.

Tips for a Demo Class

  • Practice the lesson a few times and use the materials the company provides.
  • Use gestures, facial expressions, flashcards, or a whiteboard when they help.
  • Speak clearly, pause to check understanding, and repeat key words.
  • For children, keep it playful. For adults, keep it warm and conversational.
  • Some experienced-teacher applications ask for a recording of a real class. Check what the company wants before filming.
A Personal Tip

If you're teaching kids, don't be afraid to be a little extra. 😆 I've given pretend burgers, pizza, donuts, and ice cream as rewards, sometimes with sound effects.

Kids love a big reaction. Sometimes they're more excited by your “YAAAY!” than the reward.

If you're nervous, remember: a demo is just a short class. The more relaxed and playful you are, the more comfortable your student may feel too.

Interview Tips

  • Wear something neat and professional.
  • Test your internet, camera, and microphone before the interview.
  • Keep your documents nearby in case they're requested.
  • Answer clearly and be yourself. Think about why you want to teach and which students you enjoy working with.
If You Don't Get Hired Yet

Finding the right ESL company can feel a little like dating. 😆 Sometimes you really like a company, prepare for it, and imagine yourself working there—then they reject you. Ouch.

Then a company you weren't expecting may give you a chance. Rejection happens, even when you're qualified. Take what you learned from each application and keep going when you're ready. Your next one could be the right fit.

After You're Hired

Onboarding and what to expect.

A Quick Congratulations

Congratulations! 🎉

Whether you're already hired or still getting ready to apply, you've come a long way since you opened this guide.

I know the application process can be stressful, especially for your first ESL job. Interviews, demo classes, documents, and waiting for results can make anyone nervous.

Take a moment to celebrate. You're entering the ESL world. Now let's talk about what may happen next.

What Usually Happens After You're Hired

  1. 1

    Complete onboarding

    Training may include videos, reading, quizzes, mock classes, or platform tutorials. Every company does it differently.

  2. 2

    Set up your payment account

    Check which payment methods the company supports and set up your account early.

  3. 3

    Finish your teacher profile

    Add a professional photo, complete your bio, and make sure your introduction video is up to date. Students or parents may check your profile before booking.

  4. 4

    Choose a schedule you can keep

    Open the hours that work with your life. Leave time for meals, breaks, family, errands, and rest.

  5. 5

    Prepare your teaching materials

    Your company may provide everything, or you may want a few simple props or flashcards. Build your collection over time; you don't need to buy it all at once.

  6. 6

    Teach your first classes

    Your first classes may feel awkward. You might forget something, talk too fast, or replay the lesson in your head afterward. That's part of learning. Keep showing up—you'll get more comfortable.

About Trial Classes

Try your best in every trial. A good lesson may lead to a regular student, but not every trial becomes a booking.

Some students sign up weeks or months later. Some choose another teacher, and some don't come back. Don't take it personally. Focus on giving a good lesson; the result isn't always in your hands.

Setting Expectations

Bookings take time

Some teachers get booked quickly; others wait weeks or months. A slow start doesn't mean you're a bad teacher.

Busy hours vary

Evenings and weekends may be popular, but watch your own booking patterns and adjust your schedule over time.

Feedback is part of teaching

Some comments will help; others may confuse you. Take what you can use, learn from it, and keep going.

Read your contract

Before joining another company, check for exclusivity clauses or schedule restrictions.

Continue Upskilling

Getting hired is a great start, but you can keep learning as you teach. You might explore:

  • TEFL/TESOL certificates
  • IELTS or other exam preparation
  • Business English
  • Teaching young learners
  • Private tutoring skills

These may help you work with higher-level students, teach privately, apply to premium companies, or grow your income. Studying is part of teaching too—we're lifelong learners.

Pay and Teacher Levels

Some companies use rankings or teacher levels to set pay, bonuses, or profile visibility. Novakid, Polly English, and 51Talk are examples, but their rules differ and can change. Ask how each system works before counting on a higher rate.

On Preply, tutors can set and adjust their listed rates. Cambly sets tutor pay. Check current rates and fees on each platform. If you consider a higher-paying company or private students, compare the take-home pay, schedule, and booking reliability—not just the advertised rate.

Protect Your Time and Energy

This is advice I wish I had followed sooner.

Don't feel guilty for resting, taking a day off, or closing a few slots. Burnout is real. I learned the hard way that teaching well also means making room for a life outside work.

Your students deserve an energized teacher, and you deserve time to rest too.

Payments and Taxes

  • Check how and when your company pays. Methods may include PayPal, Payoneer, Wise, bank transfer, GCash, or another service.
  • Keep a record of your earnings and read your contract carefully.
  • Ask the BIR or a qualified tax professional about your tax responsibilities. Some companies may ask for a BIR Certificate of Registration.

Requirements can vary, so confirm current details with your company and the BIR.

One Last Thing

Your first few months may not look the way you imagined.

And that's okay.

Some students will stay. Some won't.

Some days you'll feel confident. Other days you'll wonder what you're doing.

Just keep teaching, learning, and improving. The experience will come.

One day, the things that used to make you nervous may feel like part of your normal routine.

Real Talk & FAQs

Let's keep it real for a moment.

A lot of people talk about the good side of ESL—working from home, flexible schedules, meeting students from different countries, and earning online.

And yes, those things are real.

But there are also things I wish someone had told me before I started.

This Industry Has No Guaranteed Stability

Companies can pause hiring.

Policies can change.

Student demand can go up or down.

Sometimes bookings slow down for reasons you can't explain.

We've seen major changes affect ESL companies in China and the teachers who worked with them.

I'm not saying this to scare you.

I want you to start with realistic expectations. Being prepared can make changes less stressful.

What Affects Bookings?

Honestly?

Sometimes nobody really knows.

Your teaching skills, ratings, profile, availability, and student feedback can all matter.

But timing matters too.

Seasonality matters.

Platform algorithms matter.

Student demand matters.

I've seen amazing teachers struggle with bookings. I've also seen new teachers become fully booked surprisingly fast.

A slow week doesn't automatically mean you're a bad teacher. A full schedule doesn't mean you've figured everything out either.

Focus on what you can control:

  • Keep improving your teaching.
  • Show up consistently.
  • Keep your profile up to date.
  • Continue learning.

The rest is sometimes out of your hands.

For practical ideas after you're hired, see After You're Hired.

Thinking About Private Students?

A lot of teachers eventually think about finding private students.

The biggest advantage is having more control over your rates and schedule.

The trade-off?

You become the teacher, secretary, customer support, accountant, marketer, and scheduler at the same time. 😆

You find students, prepare lessons, manage payments, handle cancellations, and solve problems.

Some teachers love that freedom. Others prefer letting a company handle those things.

Neither choice is wrong.

Prepare for the Slow Months

Not every month looks the same.

Students go on vacation.

Parents get busy.

Companies change policies.

Bookings slow down.

Life happens.

If you can, try saving a little during the good months. It doesn't have to be a huge amount. Even a small emergency fund can make a slow month less stressful.

The Honest Advice

I wouldn't recommend treating ESL as your only source of income right away unless you've already built a stable student base.

It takes time.

I learned that myself.

ESL wasn't my original plan. It was more like a backup of a backup plan. Life redirected me here, and looking back, I'm grateful it did.

But I had to learn a lot along the way. If ESL is your main income, consider applying to more than one company if your contracts allow it and you can manage the schedule.

Don't put all your eggs in one basket.

A company may be your busiest platform today and your quietest one six months from now. That's part of this industry.

One Last Thing

Don't compare your journey too much with other teachers online.

Someone will always have more bookings.

Someone will always earn more.

Someone will always seem more successful.

Why I Created This Guide

The story behind everything you just read.

I was a high school teacher for seven years before I decided to leave my private school job because of an opportunity that I wanted to pursue.

As a panganay who always wanted the best for my family, I thought it was finally my chance to build a better future for us.

I spent months applying for overseas opportunities in different countries in the Middle East.

Months passed.

I waited.

Prepared.

Hoped.

Prayed.

And honestly, I had already convinced myself that I would be leaving soon.

I prepared mentally, emotionally, and financially for that next chapter of my life. I was already imagining how I could help my family more and finally achieve some of the goals I had been working so hard for.

Then the war happened.

The application process became slower and slower until eventually I started receiving deferred notices.

One.

After another.

After another.

For a while, it felt like everything was finally falling into place. There were interviews, requirements, offers, and plans for a future I thought was already waiting for me. Then everything changed.

Eventually, I decided not to pursue the opportunities anymore for the peace of mind of my family.

I was in a big slump.

At that time, I was already teaching as an ESL teacher. My first companies were Polly English and Jiaozi Tutor. Around the same time, I was also feeling burned out. I eventually resigned because I had no stable internet connection in the province and, honestly, I had no direction in life anymore.

I tried applying again, and eventually another ESL company gave me an opportunity.

When I was teaching, I was okay.

In class, I was my usual self—makwela, madaldal, and always joking around with my students. I genuinely enjoyed teaching, especially kids. Their energy made it easier to smile, laugh, and forget my problems for a little while.

But whenever I didn't have classes, especially during non-peak hours, it was a different story.

I would go back to my bed, curl up, sleep, and spend hours thinking about everything that had happened to me.

I kept replaying the situation in my head.

Wondering what went wrong.

Wondering what I could have done differently.

As a panganay, what hurt the most wasn't just losing an opportunity. It was grieving the future that I thought was already waiting for me.

My overseas application wasn't just another application. It was my biggest hope at that time. I truly believed that it would allow me to help my family more, provide a better future, and finally give back to the people who sacrificed so much for me.

So when everything suddenly stopped, it felt like the future I had been preparing for disappeared overnight.

That was probably the hardest part.

I wasn't only grieving an opportunity.

I was grieving the life that I thought was already waiting for me.

Teaching became one of the few things that distracted me from the grief. For a few hours every day, I wasn't thinking about deferred applications, failed plans, or how lost I felt. I was simply helping students learn.

Looking back now, I think teaching helped me heal little by little, even when I didn't realize it at the time.

Then something unexpected happened.

I fell in love with teaching again.

The truth is, I always loved teaching.

I was just lost, burned out, and disconnected from the passion that made me become a teacher in the first place.

Especially teaching kids.

To be honest, before entering the ESL industry, I wasn't really into teaching kids. Maybe there's a reason why life brought me here.

Maybe I also needed to improve my English-speaking skills. I may not sound like a native speaker, and I'm still far from perfect, but through consistently talking to myself, recording my voice, attending English training sessions, taking free online courses, and even talking to native speakers online just to practice, I improved little by little.

I'm still far from perfect, but I can confidently say that I've improved a lot compared to who I was before entering the ESL world.

Even before resigning from my teaching job, I always liked challenging myself and exploring what I was capable of. But after what happened with my applications, I felt completely lost.

My former colleagues and friends would often ask me what I had been doing all this time. It probably looked like I was hiding from everyone. But the truth was, I was just busy grieving.

Since I didn't have enough time to explain everything to every person who asked, I decided to create this guide.

I first started building it on Google Sites.

Then I thought, why not improve it and make it even better?

At the same time, I started building things again. Little by little, I created resources I wished I had when I was starting out.

Creating this guide gave me something to look forward to. It became one of the reasons I was excited to get up in the morning, face my laptop, and keep going instead of staying in bed and overthinking everything.

It reminded me that I still had a purpose.

That I could still share what I learned from the ESL industry.

That I could still help people who were just starting out.

Sounds dramatic, pero yun talaga. This guide became a light during a time when I felt completely lost.

At first, this guide was simply my way of answering the same questions that my former colleagues, friends, and aspiring ESL teachers kept asking me. But as I continued building it, it became something more.

It became part of my healing process.

It gave me something to work on.

Something to improve.

Something to look forward to. And for the first time in a long while, I found myself excited again.
